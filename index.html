How the study works

A Stronger Kind of Evidence

Most studies compare different people in different places. This one compares the same person at different times — a far more robust approach.

Scotland's Complete Maternity Records

Every hospital birth in Scotland between 2000 and 2015 was recorded — 841,252 pregnancies. Each record includes whether the mother smoked during pregnancy, her residential postcode, and her age, deprivation level, and urban/rural classification.

These records were linked to the Scottish Tobacco Retailers Register: a georeferenced list of every licensed tobacco outlet. Using kernel density estimation, each postcode was assigned a density value capturing the distance-weighted number of tobacco shops within 800 metres.

Key strength: Full population coverage means no sampling bias and sufficient statistical power for the analysis method used.

Why Standard Studies Fall Short

A simple cross-sectional analysis would compare different mothers living in different neighbourhoods. But this creates a confounding problem: deprived areas tend to have both more tobacco shops and higher smoking rates, for entirely separate reasons.

Even with statistical adjustment, it's nearly impossible to rule out the possibility that retailers locate where demand is already high — the opposite causal direction to what we want to test.

Confounding: Is it "more shops → more smoking", or "more demand → more shops"? Cross-sectional studies cannot distinguish between these.

Fixed-Effects: Each Mother as Her Own Control

The key innovation is comparing the same mother across her different pregnancies. When a mother moves between pregnancies, her neighbourhood tobacco density changes. Her smoking status is tracked before and after.

Because we're comparing the same person to themselves, all stable individual characteristics — personality, genes, socioeconomic background — are automatically controlled for. Only time-varying factors can confound.

Result: 55,234 pregnancies to 22,927 mothers who moved and changed their smoking status. 68% moved at least once across pregnancies.

In plain language: A pregnant woman living in a neighbourhood with the highest tobacco retailer density is around 67% more likely to be smoking during pregnancy than she would be if she lived in an area with no tobacco retailers nearby — controlling for all her individual characteristics that don't change over time.

These findings constitute "the strongest evidence to date" of an association between neighbourhood tobacco availability and smoking (Clemens et al., 2020).

Measuring exposure

What Is Tobacco Outlet Density?

The paper uses kernel density estimation (KDE) to assign each home address a tobacco exposure score. This often puzzles audiences. Here is a plain-English explanation.

📍

What it measures

A single number summarising how many tobacco shops are near each home, with closer shops weighted more heavily than distant ones. Every home in Scotland gets its own score based on its unique local retail landscape.

⚖️

Why not just count shops?

A simple count treats a shop 10m from your door identically to one 790m away. KDE captures that proximity matters — a shop right outside your door is a daily cue; one at the far edge of an 800m boundary barely registers.

🔥

The heat-source analogy

Think of each tobacco shop as a heat source. Your home's exposure score is the combined warmth you feel — shops right next door heat the space intensely; shops far away barely raise the temperature. KDE formalises this intuition.

How Distance Shapes the Score

The quartic kernel function used in the paper. The y-axis shows the contribution weight a shop at a given distance adds to the home's density score. Beyond 800m — roughly a 10-minute walk — shops contribute nothing.

Coloured dots show Shops A–E from the example above. The contrast between Shop A (close, high weight) and Shop E (distant, near-zero weight) is the key distinction KDE captures that a simple count misses.

Continuous analysis

Dose–Response Relationship

When retailer density is modelled as a continuous variable using a fractional polynomial function, the shape of the relationship becomes visible: risk increases steeply at lower densities and continues rising, but more gradually, at higher densities.

Odds ratio (y-axis) relative to zero retailer density, plotted against continuous tobacco retailer density (outlets per km²). Shaded band shows 95% confidence interval. At the highest observed density (~35 outlets/km²): OR 1.67 (1.27–2.20). Curve modelled as a fractional polynomial (square-root function) fitted to paper-reported endpoints.
